📜 Daily Poems

Poem I — Come Up from the Fields Father

Walt Whitman · 🔗 Poetry Foundation

Whitman opens on a ripe Ohio harvest — deepening green trees, apples and grapes heavy on the vine, a calm transparent sky — before a farm family receives a letter that their son Pete has been shot in the war. The poem tracks the mother’s collapse from hope (“he will soon be better”) to the unspoken truth that he won’t, and into a grief so total she withdraws from food and sleep, longing only to join him.

Why today? Early September still carries that late-summer harvest light Whitman is painting — a good hinge for a poem that uses abundance to set up how sharply loss can cut through it.

Poem II — Famous

Naomi Shihab Nye · 🔗 Poetry Foundation

Nye redefines fame at human scale — a river “famous to” the fish that swim it, a pulley “famous to” the person who relies on it, a pair of boots “famous to the earth” through years of wear. She lands on wanting to be remembered the way ordinary, steadfast things are: useful, present, quietly relied upon rather than celebrated.

Pairing: Where Whitman finds a family’s whole world upended by one letter, Nye finds meaning in the small and unnoticed — together they frame a day’s worth of attention.

🎨 Art of the Day

George Tooker
“I am after reality — painting impressed on the mind so hard that it recurs as a dream... but I am not after dreams as such, or fantasy.”

John Steuart Curry · Wisconsin Landscape (1938–39)

🔗 View at The Met · Oil on canvas

Curry was one of the three great Regionalist painters (alongside Grant Wood and Thomas Hart Benton) who turned away from European modernism to paint the American Midwest on its own terms. This rolling Wisconsin farm scene is quieter than his famous Kansas storm paintings, but carries the same conviction that ordinary rural land was subject worthy of serious painting.

George Tooker · Landscape with Figures III (1989)

🔗 View at The Met · Tempera on wood panel

Tooker is the defining American magic realist — a painter of crowded subways, waiting rooms, and government offices rendered with an eerie, dreamlike stillness that reads as ordinary life slightly out of joint. This later landscape carries his signature tempera precision into open space rather than his usual claustrophobic interiors.
